Start Here

A working reference for the Station Commander's Course — a live MAP instrument you can actually build a plan in, plus the doctrine behind it, cited to paragraph level.

This is built around a simple premise: the Mission Accomplishment Plan is the thing a station commander is judged on, and it is the thing most likely to be tested, but almost nobody is taught to read it. The manuals treat MAP construction as arithmetic and MAP analysis as an afterthought. In practice the arithmetic is done for you by Recruiter Zone. The analysis is not.

So the MAP Builder here is not a worksheet that reproduces a form. It builds a full plan — station or individual — from your own conversion data, then lets you enter what actually happened week by week and tells you where the funnel is leaking, how badly, and what UTC 5–03.1's own troubleshooting guide says to do about it.

The instrument

Four steps: set the mission and calendar, enter or derive conversion ratios, generate the MAP with weekly distribution and per-recruiter allocation, then track achievement and read the chokepoints.

The doctrine

Six chapters covering MAP theory, the operations process, and decisive/shaping/sustaining operations — written as explanation, not as a list of paragraph numbers to memorize.

MAP Theory & Analysis

What the Mission Accomplishment Plan actually is, how it is built, how it lies to you when your data is dirty, and how to read a chokepoint before it costs you the month.

The definition you need cold

UM 3–31 defines the MAP as a mathematical tool that provides station commanders with a glide path and predictor mechanism to understand how much effort is required to achieve the monthly mission, and states that it allows station commanders to operationalize work effort into actions within the station recruiting plan. UTC 5–03.1 adds the second half: the MAP represents the amount of activity a unit must generate to achieve its mission, based on historical conversion data.

Both halves matter. The first tells you what it does — predict. The second tells you what it is made of — your own history. That second half is the source of nearly every way the MAP goes wrong.

Two purposes, stated explicitly

The MAP is a living document with two main purposes. First, it serves as an early warning system to detect chokepoints within the prospecting and processing cycles. Second, it identifies leadership and training challenges. para 10–5

What it is not

It is not the station recruiting plan. The plan is a five-paragraph OPORD covering lead generation, prospecting, processing, and leading Future Soldiers; the MAP is a set of numbers that validates whether that plan is adequate. UM 3–31 calls the MAP a litmus test of the operation plan: if the actions inside the operation do not meet or exceed the requirements inside the MAP, mission failure is highly probable.

It is also not a substitute for leadership, and the regulation is unusually blunt about this. Paragraph 10–4 closes with "it is important to remember the MAP is only a guide and never replaces good leadership." Paragraph 10–5 goes further: in successful units, the MAP does not determine the work efforts. Units that treat prospecting as a routine daily event generally surpass their MAP goals without reference to them. The MAP is what you reach for when that is not happening.

A leader who cannot explain the science used to formulate MAP goals is unable to convince subordinates why they should meet them. UTC 5–03.1, para 10–3

Reading the published recruiting calendar

Two markers on the calendar drive every date in the MAP, and the thing that trips people is that they refer to different months. The purple T–2 Begins cell in a month's row starts prospecting for the next recruiting month. The red End of RSM cell in a month's row ends that month.

Doctrine's DEC FY24 example proves it three times over: T–2 for December is marked in the November row at day 20, giving "Monday, 20 NOV" (para 10–15); December's own End of RSM sits at day 29, giving "Friday, 29 DEC" (para 10–19); and the T–2 marked in the December row at day 18 is the one the manual calls "the next T–2" — January's.

Holidays are marked as bands, not single days

This is where the day counts come from. Para 10–15 notes that "23-26 NOV is marked as the Thanksgiving holiday on the calendar," so the T–2 prospecting week of 20–25 NOV is truncated to 20–22 NOV — three days, not four. The band takes Thursday, Friday and Saturday. Counting only the federal holiday date would leave you one day over, and one day over at the top of the funnel compounds all the way down.

The five conventions

These are mechanical rules, and they are the most reliably tested part of MAP construction.

1 · Always round up

Where rounding applies, round up and never down. A weekly requirement of 16.5 Appointments Made becomes 17. You cannot recruit a fraction of a person.

para 10–7a

2 · Distribute, don't just divide

Weekly whole numbers must sum back to the monthly total. A monthly 66 over four weeks at 16.5 each becomes 17 / 17 / 16 / 16. Load the higher requirement earlier unless that produces something unrealistic.

para 10–7b

3 · Never zero

No week should carry a zero requirement while there is an available recruiting force. Prospecting, processing, and leading Future Soldiers run continuously.

para 10–7c

4 · Only Alphas are named

The RA MAP tracks NPS mental category I–IIIA only — Grad Alpha and Senior Alpha. Lower categories, non-grads, non-seniors, and prior service all roll into OTH. The AR MAP additionally names prior service.

para 10–7d/e

5 · Cover OTH through Bravo fallout

This is the convention people get wrong. Because only Alphas are named, the OTH mission has to come from somewhere — and doctrine's answer is that it comes from the IIIB testers who fall out of Alpha prospecting. Grad Bravo and Senior Bravo conversion ratios tell you how much OTH your planned Alpha prospecting will incidentally produce.

The regulation's own worked example: a station with 400 grad Appointments Made producing 20 GA and 10 GB contracts, and 300 senior AM producing 10 SA and 15 SB, has a GA conversion of 20:1, GB of 40:1, SA of 30:1, and SB of 20:1. Missioned 3 GA / 2 SA / 3 OTH, it plans 60 grad AM (3 × 20) and 60 senior AM (2 × 30). Those 60 grad AM at 40:1 yield 1 GB; the 60 senior AM at 20:1 yield 3 SB. Total projected: 3 GA, 2 SA, and 4 OTH — mission covered with one to spare.

When the fallout does not cover OTH, doctrine says to use the better of your GA or SA conversion and add appointments to the stronger category rather than prospecting OTH directly — you will overproduce an Alpha contract, which takes precedence over OTH anyway. para 10–7

Where conversion data comes from — and how it rots

Conversion data is the average number of Appointments Made to interviews conducted, applicants tested to number passed, and applicants physically examined to those passed, required to achieve a single enlistment contract. RA data is organized as graduate (GRD), senior (SR), and other (OTH); AR data adds prior service (PS). para 10–8

Accurate data takes time. A leader inheriting a unit with unrealistic numbers can request higher-headquarters data to "norm" against; below 90 days, stations use the company average. Reasonable unit-specific data generally emerges after about 90 days. And doctrine states the cause plainly: high conversion data is usually the result of a lack of training or poor leadership. para 10–9

The manipulation trap — worth understanding, not just memorizing

Conversion ratios can be driven down in a way that looks like efficiency but is actually rot. Suppose the auto-calculated MAP shows a 3:1 Appointments Made to contract ratio in GRD. That looks superb. But if the unit has consistently failed to hit its requirements from Appointments Made through Test Passed while converting very efficiently from Test Passed to Contract, the top of the funnel has simply stopped being populated. Chronic under-achievement at the top drove GRD conversion from 8:1 down to 3:1 — and if the leader auto-populates the MAP from that number, the resulting requirements are far too small to make mission. para 10–10

Two related failure modes. Miscoding: entering prospects as grads or seniors who later turn out to be non-grads or dropouts artificially inflates future prospecting goals. Re-norming: when data becomes unrealistic, commanders often lower goals to achievable levels — which doctrine calls a two-edged sword, because a goal reduction can cost you visibility of a systemic problem and rarely produces mission unless intense training accompanies it. Once conversion becomes unrealistic it takes considerable time to recover, and in the meantime the recruiters' confidence in the MAP deteriorates, which makes it useless as a tool. paras 10–35, 10–36

The station's success is in meeting the requirements on the bottom of the MAP, not the top. UTC 5–03.1, para 10–36

Chokepoints

A chokepoint is a stoppage between processing steps — when activity in one step is sufficient to generate activity in the next, but the expected activity does not develop. Doctrine's example: a station that normally conducts 68% of its appointments dropping to 60% in a given week indicates a chokepoint between Appointments Made and Appointments Conducted. para 10–39

The analytical move is always the same: compare against the benchmark, then determine whether the cause is leadership or recruiting skill, and take appropriate action. But doctrine also warns about reading percentages naively. If grad appointments conducted runs at 90% — well above the 75% benchmark — the first question is whether the number is meaningful. Ninety percent of 425 Appointments Made is significant; 90% of 50 may not be. The second question is whether it is even accurate: verify results when a recruiter reports an accomplishment that greatly exceeds goal. If accurate, train everyone on the method that produced it. If not, correct the reporting immediately, because the longer an inaccuracy persists the more it corrupts conversion data. para 10–40

The high-conversion paradox

In doctrine's own example, that 90% conducts rate made the next step harder: against a 36% Appointments-Conducted-to-Test benchmark, the station was only testing 22% of grads and 20% of seniors from appointments conducted. Doctrine's conclusion is that a training plan correcting that single shortfall — conducted to test — could produce a large gain in station performance without increasing any recruiter's workload. para 10–40

Using conversion data to predict, mid-month

This is the most practically useful technique in the chapter and the least taught. Because conversion data is a ratio, you can run it forward at any point in the month to determine what should already have happened.

  • If 82% of Appointments Made convert to Appointments Conducted and the station has made 52 appointments, roughly 42.6 should have been conducted. If they were not, identify which recruiters are affected and personally work the no-show follow-ups.
  • If Appointments Made to Contract runs 17:1, then 1 ÷ 17 = 5.8% of Appointments Made become enlistments. From 52 Appointments Made, about 3 people should already have enlisted or be near the end of processing. If those three did not appear, they are somewhere in the pipeline waiting on follow-up.
  • If the station conducted 58 interviews at a 14:1 conduct-to-contract ratio, it should have enlisted about 4. Doctrine's framing: if it did not, they are out there, waiting for the right follow-up — the hard work of generating appointments is already done.

The leadership payoff is explicit: a station commander who directs recruiters toward results that do not require additional prospecting gains credibility, and managing conversion upward removes the need for more prospecting, which would only inflate conversion data in later months. paras 10–41 to 10–46

Two behaviors that quietly destroy months

Sandbagging

Holding enlistments to posture for the next month. It can mean a station never reaches its full potential: applicants often read an enlistment delay as a break in trust and may not enlist at all, and recruiters who believe next month is already in the bag take prospecting lightly. Every applicant ready to enlist — especially in the last week — should do so.

para 10–48

Stove piping

Enlisting prospects generated for the next month into the current one. It compresses the pipeline and negates T–2 and T–1 prospecting. Leaders must identify when it happens and increase prospecting to compensate, using MAP conversion data to calculate how many additional appointments the next month now needs.

para 10–52

Both belong to a larger pattern doctrine describes: in the final two weeks of any month the station is running two distinct operations at once — processing the current month's enlistments and prospecting for the next month's mission. They are difficult to separate, and the prospecting half is the one that gets sacrificed. para 10–51

Compare the MAP to a nuclear facility. Failure to react to warning signs could result in a complete meltdown. Alarms at a nuclear reactor are loud and highly visible, whereas the alarms associated with the MAP are silent. UTC 5–03.1, para 10–50

The Operations Process

Command and control, the eight recruiting functions, troop leading procedures, and the analytical frameworks that turn a market into a plan.

Command and control, and mission command

Command and control is the exercise of authority and direction by a properly designated commander over assigned and attached forces in accomplishing a mission. Mission command is the Army's approach to command and control — the one that empowers subordinate decision-making. The distinction matters on tests: C2 is the warfighting function, mission command is the philosophy for exercising it.

UM 3–31 lists seven mission command principles; UM 3–0 lists eight, adding "empower subordinates." Know both lists and where each comes from.

Mission command principles

Competence · Mutual trust · Shared understanding · Commander's intent · Mission orders · Disciplined initiative · Risk acceptance · Empower subordinates (UM 3–0 only)

UM 3–31 1–21; UM 3–0 3–8

The four C2 components

People — the chain of command, from XOs and OICs down to station commanders and recruiters. Processes — the eight recruiting functions and established SOPs. Networks — Recruiter Zone, IKROme, and the internal/external networks. Command posts — battalion and company HQ, recruiting stations, and wherever leaders place themselves during operations.

UM 3–0, para 3–9

The eight recruiting functions

These are recruiting's analogue to the Army's six warfighting functions — a common organization for critical tasks. Command and control sits at the center and drives the other seven. Doctrine's warning is that units able to execute all eight simultaneously to standard are capable of sustained excellence, while failure in one or more creates conditions for challenges down the road.

1 · Command & Control

Fundamental to all operations; drives the other seven.

2 · Intelligence & Market Analysis

Understand market, competition, and OE before operating in them.

3 · Lead Generation & Prospecting

Generate names, then contact them. Prospecting is the most important daily task.

4 · Interviewing

The Army Interview — the centerpiece all other functions support or result from.

5 · Processing

Begins the moment a prospect says yes.

6 · Leading Future Soldiers

Sustain commitment, keep them qualified, prepare them for training.

7 · Training & Leader Development

Institutional, operational, and self-development domains.

8 · Sustainment

Human resources, logistics, and information technology.

Work ethic is the underpinning

UM 3–0 flags work ethic as "one of the primary underpinnings of proper execution of all recruiting functions." In recruiting specifically, work ethic is the daily prospecting and processing effort toward accomplishing the mission using all available resources. Individual work ethic is the keystone of the USAREC mission and is captured through systems discipline at recruiter and station level; the collective work ethic of an organization determines mission success at every echelon. UM 3–0 3–2/3–3; UM 3–31 1–20

Understand → Visualize → Describe → Direct → Lead → Assess

The recruiting operations process mirrors the Army's. The four command and control activities performed during operations are planning, preparing, executing, and continuously assessing; the station commander drives that process through six verbs.

Understand

Use PMESII-PT and METT-TC to read the local environment — the demographic makeup of the AO, the needs and motivations of the market, and what messages will generate interest. This assessment is what identifies threats and opportunities and drives mitigating action.

Visualize

The Recruiting Functions Analysis and the SWOT analysis are how visualization actually happens. The RFA gives a systematic method to assess station performance across all eight recruiting functions and surfaces trends, gaps, and obstacles; it tells you about the station's proficiency with critical recruiting tasks and the effectiveness of current systems and processes. Both RFA and SWOT are running estimates — continuously maintained, not one-time products.

Describe

Build shared understanding between commander and recruiters about the OE, the mission, and the commander's intent, then task-organize the force to finalize the station recruiting plan.

Direct

Direct all aspects of station operations, adapting as new information arrives. Know the force well enough to leverage each recruiter's individual strengths across lead generation, prospecting, processing, and leading Future Soldiers.

Lead

Identify the points of decisive action in the plan and be physically present at them. Leaders who personally observe execution can evaluate individual recruiter effectiveness, which is what makes coaching credible — and it demonstrates commitment to each recruiter's development.

Assess

Assess continuously using measures of performance and measures of effectiveness. Progress reviews assess current operations, a specific mission or event, or individual performance; AARs assess previous operations against an established standard, asking what happened, why it happened, and how to improve. Station commanders are responsible for conducting weekly AARs, during which they review the MAP and prospecting requirements, the FSTP, and the station recruiting plan against actual accomplishments to develop a COA for the coming week.

MOP vs. MOE — a reliable test question

Measure of performance: an indicator to gauge how well an action is executed. Measure of effectiveness: an indicator used to measure change in a current system state, by comparing multiple observations over time. Concretely: running two packet-QC coaching sessions as planned is a MOP; a subsequent drop in packet returns is an MOE. UM 3–31 glossary; JP 5–0

The three analytical frameworks

These get confused with each other constantly. The clean distinction: PMESII-PT is the widest lens on the environment, METT-TC is the mission-planning lens, and ASCOPE is a sub-tool that breaks down the civil-considerations variable inside METT-TC.

Recruiting zones

Assigning zones well gives recruiters a fair, stable market they can develop, nurture, and penetrate — which is what produces school ownership and community familiarity. Recruiters as sensors

Recruiters possess information that can change the leader's understanding of the situation, and doctrine treats them explicitly as sensors under "Every Soldier a Sensor." Station commanders facilitate collection through daily interactions, IPRs, and AARs, and stations apply ASCOPE to pass information from sensors up to leaders. Open-source information comes from any person or group providing it without an expectation of privacy; publicly available information is material published or broadcast for general consumption, available on request, lawfully seen or heard by any casual observer, or made available at a public meeting. Decisive Operations

Prospecting, the Army Interview, processing, the in-progress review, and leading Future Soldiers — the operations that directly produce the mission.

Decisive operations directly support mission accomplishment. The station commander is responsible for a station recruiting plan that unifies decisive, shaping, and sustaining operations, and uses the electronic planning guide to validate individual recruiter plans and provide direction.

Prospecting

Prospecting is the most important daily task of recruiting. UM 3–31, para 3–2

Well-planned and executed prospecting is the most reliable way to build the number of quality prospects necessary to achieve mission. It puts recruiters in direct contact with leads, influencers, and community partners. There are three methods, and the station commander's job is to optimize the mix for the environment and target market — then guide recruiters toward specific markets: high school seniors, graduates, medical students, seminarians, or Soldiers in targeted MOSs.

Telephone

Flexible — a recruiter can switch markets mid-session. Texting is a subset. Success requires in-depth research and blueprinting from Future Soldiers, social media, yearbooks, local papers, influencers, and professional organizations. Station commanders actively monitor for call reluctance and demonstrate how detailed preparation increases effectiveness.

3–7 / 3–8

Face-to-face

The most direct form and the most resource-intensive. Good plans start from lead clusters — people who live, work, or congregate together. If the target is unavailable, capitalize on the influencers present: parents, friends, coaches, coworkers. With proper planning it is often the station's most effective method and yields the best contact-to-contract ratio.

3–9 to 3–11

Virtual

Email, social media, and other digital means. Highly efficient at reaching volume, and most effective in addition to other methods rather than instead of them. Messaging must stay consistent with Army Values and current social media policy. Particularly useful for in-service and medical recruiting.

3–12 to 3–14

The Army Interview

Doctrine calls the Army Interview the centerpiece of all recruiting activities — UM 3–0 goes further and says all recruiting functions either support it or are the direct result of it. Formally, it is a meeting between recruiter and prospect to tell the Army story and counsel them on the benefits of enlistment or commission.

The station commander's role here is specific and personal: you are the subject matter expert and primary trainer within the station. You are responsible for assessing each recruiter's ability to conduct an effective interview and providing feedback, and for developing interview skills to improve the ability to engender a commitment to serve. Recruiters must connect on a personal level and tell the Army story in a way that is meaningful, understandable, and convincing.

UM 3–0 adds why the recruiter's own story carries the weight it does: the recruiter's personal narrative — enlistment, assignments, promotions, awards, campaigns, education, locations — lets a prospect see the Army through the recruiter's eyes and picture themselves as a Soldier. The most effective and decisive voice telling the Army story is the Soldier who wears the Army Recruiter Badge.

Processing

Processing starts when a prospect agrees to join — enlist, commission, or reclassify. That can happen during the interview, during a follow-up, or even after testing. It is a series of administrative tasks: completing the enlistment application and validating aptitude, moral, and medical qualifications.

The station commander is the driving force behind processing and manages all timelines, and remains involved from initial commitment through completion of the Future Soldier Training Program. Validation happens through daily IPRs and quality control checks.

The in-progress review

The IPR is a systematic, step-by-step review of information between echelons — at station level, an in-depth dialog between the station commander and an individual recruiter about current operations or preparation for upcoming events. It can be conducted face-to-face, by telephone, or on Teams.

Not a "sometime" event

UM 3–30 states it directly: the IPR is not a "sometime" event, but an "everyday" event. It identifies the status of current operations and provides immediate guidance to achieve daily and weekly objectives. UM 3–31 calls it an essential daily station commander task that drives production. UM 3–30 4–40; UM 3–31 3–18

To run one effectively, doctrine says you must possess and have mastered five things: a complete understanding of UM 3–30 and 3–31 as appropriate, the company ROP, the station recruiting plan, access to Recruiter Zone, and access to and understanding of the MAP. Preparation means researching the data across all seven topics beforehand so you arrive with situational understanding rather than building it during the conversation.

IPR versus AAR

The continuous operational tempo of recruiting is why USAREC runs both: a daily IPR and a weekly AAR. The IPR looks at current and near-term operations; the AAR looks backward at a completed operation or event against an established standard and asks three questions — what happened, why did it happen, and how can we improve next time.

Doctrine's argument for why AARs matter is about ownership rather than record-keeping: when recruiters share in identifying the reasons for success and failure, they become owners of it. AARs also give leaders an opportunity to hear what is actually on their team members' minds. No leader, however skilled, sees as much as an entire team performing the mission.

Leading Future Soldiers

The station commander is responsible for the FSTP. For the enlisted mission it prepares new members for the rigors of initial military training and life as a Soldier — mentally, physically, and emotionally — through leadership and counseling, physical fitness training, and formal Army distributed learning, while sustaining commitment.

Future Soldiers are also an operational asset: they assist in decisive and shaping operations by providing referrals and blueprint information that refines existing leads, and they accompany recruiters on area canvassing, face-to-face prospecting, ARSOF briefings, and community partner events.

Two hard requirements to know: station commanders validate that Future Soldier records are complete before the pre-ship QC window, 7 to 30 days from shipping, and ensure Future Soldiers continue to meet enlistment eligibility from entry into DEP/DTP until shipping. After each Future Soldier function, conduct an AAR with recruiters and Future Soldiers, build on successes, ask for referrals, and complete UF 601–210.26.

Future Soldier losses

Not all losses are preventable — life circumstances and medical conditions can make someone temporarily or permanently ineligible. But doctrine states plainly that many Future Soldier losses are avoidable, and names the avoidable categories: law violations, failure to meet physical standards, apathy, and failure to graduate. Station commanders work with recruiters to identify and mitigate those, submit the loss request form and notify the commander immediately where a loss is in the Army's best interest, and identify loss trends in order to redirect prospecting toward markets with better-qualified applicants. A Future Soldier who is lost may still support the effort through referrals.

Shaping Operations

Lead generation, planning, battle rhythm, the station recruiting plan and synch matrix, and the School Recruiting Program — the work that makes next month's decisive operations possible.

Shaping operations create the conditions that support decisive operations. They are deliberate, not incidental: planned in advance to maximize the return on recruiters' effort, and tracked in the station recruiting plan, the EPGs, and the company's synch matrix and ROP. As the station recruiting plan develops, shaping operations get refined to support it and increase return on investment.

Lead generation, referrals, and refinement

Leads enable prospecting. Lead generation is any activity to obtain the names and contact information of individuals who may have an interest in the Army — executed through area canvassing, referrals, and virtual and physical marketing campaigns. The station commander uses intelligence and market analysis to direct it toward clearly defined, decisive, and attainable objectives.

A distinction worth being precise about

Referrals are a powerful lead source but are not a form of prospecting. A referral is a lead provided by a pre-existing member of a recruiter's internal or external network. Prospecting is an action a recruiter takes to contact a lead. Conflating them on a test is an easy loss. UM 3–31, paras 4–3/4–4

Lead refinement is a critical step performed by recruiters, station leadership, and higher-echelon USAREC support such as the call center; station commanders provide the refinement guidance. It involves reducing large lists to smaller focused lists, leader-to-subordinate discussion of leads during IPRs, verifying initial interest and eligibility, ongoing blueprinting, and rapport building for follow-up.

Planning

Planning is the art and science of understanding the situation, envisioning a desired future, and developing effective ways of bringing that future about. The station commander plans with recruiters using bottom-up feedback; the company commander validates the resulting plan; and the plan is then what establishes priorities and justifies resource requests.

The plan is built along four areas of focus — lead generation, prospecting, processing, and leading Future Soldiers — and built by backward planning from requirements to resources. The station commander then validates that recruiters' individual plans are nested with the station plan, so that effort is unified rather than parallel.

Battle rhythm and the weekly planning meeting

Battle rhythm is a deliberate cycle of command, staff, and unit activities to synchronize current and future operations, conducted on a recurring basis to set conditions for success. The purpose at station level is specific: create consistency and predictability, and increase operational efficiency.

The weekly planning meeting is where that rhythm does its work. Its job is to synchronize station prospecting, processing, and Future Soldier operations. The station commander reviews and compares current prospecting results against the MAP and modifies future prospecting accordingly, then assesses applicant issues, Future Soldier issues, upcoming events, and resource needs, adjusting the station recruiting plan as necessary.

Separately, the leader huddle is a regularly scheduled meeting through the month involving station leadership — generally the station commander and ASC — to share updates, discuss operations, and keep leadership aligned on purpose, direction, goals, and priorities. UM 3–31, para 1–23

The station recruiting plan

The plan encompasses the four areas of focus and unifies decisive, shaping, and sustaining operations. Doctrine's standard for a good one: agile and straightforward, accounting for uncertainty while mitigating risk.

Its initial input is the commander's intent and targeting guidance — a clear, concise statement of what the force must do and the conditions required to achieve the mission. The station commander then describes the conditions and limits within which subordinates may exercise initiative while maintaining unity of effort.

Target identification draws on IPOE knowledge and personal experience plus every available source: battalion S–2 analysis, historical market data, and bottom-up input from recruiters. If resource requirements exceed what the station holds, request from the company commander as early as possible to ensure availability.

The synch matrix

Used alongside the plan, the synch matrix enables the station commander to synchronize available resources and request additional ones in support of the overarching targeting process. Station commanders are responsible for providing input to the company synch matrix, and company commanders have the authority to direct station-level synch matrices when necessary.

The School Recruiting Program

Establishing an SRP is vital because secondary and postsecondary schools account for the majority of enlistments and commissions. The station commander is responsible for developing the SRP and enforcing completion of its tasks, and for ensuring recruiters understand its specific requirements.

The validation piece is concrete and testable: station commanders validate that recruiters build school events in the school folders, conduct the scheduled events, and document actions in School Zone and EPGs. School data itself is a running estimate — it identifies students who have taken the ASVAB, those who enlisted RA or USAR, and ROTC participants, and the station commander analyzes it to develop the school recruiting plan.

Detailed SRP planning considerations live in UTC 5–03.1 and UR 350–13.

Sustaining Operations & Networks

Logistics, personnel, training and leader development, and the three-part network that forms the station's information environment.

Sustaining operations enable decisive and shaping operations by providing market and recruiter support. They are inseparable from the other two but are not usually decisive themselves, and doctrine's framing of their importance is about duration: sustaining operations determine how long the recruiting force can exploit successful decisive and shaping operations. Failure to sustain harms market penetration, recruiter success, and mission accomplishment.

Logistics and advertising assets

The company provides the bulk of logistics support. The station commander maintains facilities, vehicles, cell phones, and computer equipment; repair or replacement usually requires coordination with battalion.

On advertising assets, the standard is anticipatory rather than reactive: ensure recruiters have an ample supply of RPI and PPI, evaluate all planned events to confirm adequate stock levels, and use the online ordering system to maintain them. Recruiters use these to create follow-up opportunities and support special events.

Personnel management and integration

Doctrine's language here is deliberate: station commanders are responsible for the command's most important resource: the recruiter. That means knowing and caring for the welfare and professional development of the force, and maintaining the station's leave program, medical readiness, military and civilian education, family welfare, and holistic health and fitness.

Integration of a new recruiter has specific steps: assist with in-processing using UF 350–1.4 per UR 350–1; familiarize them with the station; give a physical overview of the AO and individual recruiting zones; accompany them on their first visit to assigned schools; provide initial counseling on general and job-specific procedures, requirements, and expectations; then submit the completed form to battalion trainers to initiate the Advanced Training Program.

Training and leader development

The station commander is in charge of the station's training and responsible for ensuring completion of leader development programs. Development starts with the Reception and Integration Program; the Advanced Training Program in UR 350–1 then validates foundational skills and focuses training only on the new recruiter's deficiencies.

The three training domains are institutional (schools), operational (unit training), and self-development. Station training and leader development sit in the operational domain, and the station commander plays the critical role in assessing proficiencies and identifying training needs.

Mechanically: station training plans are developed from the quarterly CSTAR (Company/Station Training Assessment Review), which identifies weekly training sessions and initiates required refresher training. Near-term training is captured on UF 350–1.1 and covers one month within the short-term quarterly plan. Individual training is documented on UF 350–1.2 and maintained in ATIS, the Army Training Information System.

System-of-record change to watch

UM 3–31 para 5–14 specifies ATIS for individual training records. Older material and habit will point you at DTMS. If a test question asks where individual training records are maintained under the current manual, the answer is ATIS. UM 3–31, para 5–14

Doctrine also carries a note worth remembering as a principle rather than a fact: verbal communication and written counseling foster a learning environment, and the NCOER drives the process of professional development in subordinate leaders.

The three sustainment pillars

Human resources

Appropriate staffing in line with approved force structure and positioning analysis. Station commanders monitor and maintain personnel and medical readiness, safety, and H2F to care for Soldiers and Families.

Logistical

Procurement and maintenance across all facets of logistical support — request and delivery of equipment, supplies, resources, vehicles, transportation coordination, and travel.

Information & technology

What enables a station commander to operate across a geographically dispersed AO: technology-based networks, information systems, mobility including functionality and security, and equipment provision.

The assessment questions doctrine offers for sustainment operations are a usable checklist for a weekly planning meeting: do recruiters have the resources to support targeted events; has anyone scheduled leave; are there Soldier or Family issues; are any government-owned vehicles due for maintenance; do recruiters have enough RPI and PPI for planned events?

Networks

Network-centric operations in recruiting focus primarily at company level and below, and the recruiting station is the focal point of the recruiting network. The network has three parts — internal, external, and information systems — and together they form the station's information environment: the individuals, organizations, and systems that contribute, process, and disseminate the information a station commander needs for operational advantage.

Internal network

Company and battalion headquarters providing administrative, operational, and logistical support. When those assets are integrated into the station recruiting plan, the internal network positively influences the target market and generates leads. It also includes the Recruiting ProNet Archive — more than 15 years of TTPs, lessons learned, templates, how-tos, video case studies, and USAR and virtual recruiting material.

External network

Secondary and postsecondary schools and military units are the primary lead sources, and they contain the community partners that matter: students, guidance counselors, teachers, financial advisors, unit leaders, and military school staff. External sources improve the Army's credibility, provide leads in target markets, and create additional market opportunities.

Beyond schools: local organizations, businesses, and government leaders. Doctrine names the specific civic organizations where recruiters and leaders commonly participate — Veterans of Foreign Wars, the American Legion, the local chamber of commerce, Rotary Club, Kiwanis, and Lions Club — because participation puts recruiters in contact with potential community partners. Local newspapers, radio, and television can also be leveraged to promote the Army.

For in-service recruiting, schools and courses within The Army School System are external-network lead sources that let the station commander build a list of targeted MOSs with higher propensity for success, such as Airborne Infantry and Rangers.

Information systems

An information system is equipment that collects, processes, stores, displays, and disseminates information. Named examples: IKROme, BI Zone, Recruiter Zone, School Zone, Reserve Unit Zone, and the Recruiting Market Zone Tool. Doctrine's framing is that an effective network provides a common recruiting picture analogous to a battlefield commander's common operating picture.

Systems discipline

Systems discipline is the action or inaction of complementary systems and processes that organizations use to collect, filter, process, create, and distribute data as intended. Station commanders are responsible for monitoring, validating, and ensuring compliance.

It matters for a reason that goes beyond compliance: systems discipline is what allows the station commander to understand, visualize, and direct operations at all — it provides a transparent, up-to-date snapshot at any point in the operations process while minimizing the distance problem in a geographically dispersed environment. It is also what accurately records recruiter work ethic and historical data, which is what the MAP is built from.

Community partners

Community partners are those within the community who have access to the populations and markets that influence the enlistment decision. Station commanders leverage local community leaders who agree to advocate for the Army in order to mitigate recruiters' challenges and overcome obstacles. Cultivating them is what aids the station commander in placing the recruiter in the right market at the right time. Detail lives in UTC 5–03.3.

Troubleshooting Guide

Organized by symptom. When prospecting or processing performance falls below acceptable levels, find the stage that is leaking and work the questions doctrine puts under it.

Doctrine's framing of the goal: ensure there are enough qualified people in the recruiting funnel and that they are flowing smoothly through the process. Those are two different failures with two different fixes, and the guide below is structured around which one you have.

Three reports do the diagnostic work. The Performance and Mission reports in Recruiter Zone track the process through every step. The LPA report in BI Zone lets you visualize prospecting methods from contact attempts through appointments conducted. The contact time report displays attempts and contacts by period. For in-service recruiters, the Level 1 log covers contact through brief. The point of all of them is to determine total prospecting requirements and which method works, at what time — something conduct-to-contract ratios alone cannot tell you. Doctrine adds a specific instruction: station commanders must calculate how many hours of prospecting it takes to generate a contract.

Scope

The guide is explicitly not exhaustive, but doctrine states it applies to most situations. Healthcare recruiting is excluded because each area of concentration needs unique conversion considerations — though the chokepoints and tactics from the enlisted funnel still provide useful insight. paras 11–4, 11–43

  2. In-Service OCS Information Sheet (HRC website)
  3. DA Form 5339 — OCS Applicant's Commanders Evaluation Sheet, completed by the immediate commander
  4. Typed essay, "Why I Want to be an Officer" — Arial 12, one page, 1-inch margins, header with rank/name/MI/suffix, proctored and digitally signed by the immediate commander
  5. Education Center memorandum certifying a conferred baccalaureate degree, including cumulative GPA, type of degree, and date conferred
  6. Letters of recommendation — optional, not to exceed 2, and from people other than the endorsing officials in para 3–1
  7. DA Form 5500 / 5501 — Body Fat Content Worksheet
  8. DA Form 705–Test — Army Fitness Test Scorecard
  9. ECLT verification where applicable

Separately, two Memoranda of Endorsement are required and must be the template versions from the HRC website: one from the Brigade Commander (O–6) and one from the Unit Command Sergeant Major. Both must address responsibility, leadership potential, and critical thinking as outlined in ADP 6–22, and be submitted as part of the In-Service OCS application in IPPS–A.

Removed by the 2026 revision

The requirement for DA Form 61 (Application for Appointment), DA Form 4322 (Army Officer Candidate Contract and Service Agreement), DA Form 6283 (Leadership Assessment Program), and DA Form 6285 (Interview Plan No. 2) were all removed. If older study material references them, it is out of date. The revision also added Talent Based Branching for RA In-Service candidates and an additional service obligation for choice of branch, station, and/or branch detail.

AR 190–51 — Security of Unclassified Army Resources

27 June 2019

An Army-wide physical security regulation covering arms, aircraft, petroleum, communications facilities, medical resources, museums, and USACE project resources. It never mentions recruiting by name, but a recruiting station's facility, keys and locks, government vehicles, and equipment are unclassified Army resources and fall under its minimum standards.

The framing to hold onto: actual physical security posture is based on local conditions and risk analysis, but must not be less than the minimum standards for the relevant resource category. The 2019 major revision realigned responsibility for conducting physical security inspections from facility commanders up to commanders and directors of ACOMs, ASCCs, DRUs, the CNGB, and USACE divisions, and revised policy for locks and keys (app D) and secure storage structures (app B).

AR 25–51 — Official Mail and Distribution Management

30 January 2023

Governs official mail and postage account management Army-wide. Relevant if your station handles official mail or franking; it carries no recruiting-specific provisions and few day-to-day station commander decisions. The 2023 action certified the regulation current with administrative changes only.
